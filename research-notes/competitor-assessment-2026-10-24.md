# Competitor assessment: leading personal sites in Rhett's space

Date: Oct 24, 2026. Prepared for the personal site backlog. All three sites were fetched and read live on this date. No substitution was needed. This file is repo only and is not in the deploy allowlist, so it never publishes.

Baseline for comparison: Rhett's site is one page with four sections (Introduction, Background, Outside work, Elsewhere), the headline "I work on buildings and capital decisions.", a headshot, and links to Boston Capital Strategy, LinkedIn, and X. Its one job is to make a warm introduction easier to advance. Rhett is a Consultant at Deloitte, so employer risk rules out most of what independent operators do with their sites. Every steal and skip below is judged through that lens.

## 1. Dror Poleg, drorpoleg.com

Verdict: The strongest model of the three for Rhett, because the site is an authority record rather than a shopfront, and most of its machinery (named idea, bylines, interviews, a books record) points outward to work hosted elsewhere.

- Positioning and headline: The title is "Dror Poleg, economic historian and keynote speaker on AI and work" and the hero headline is "Charting AI's impact on work". One named territory, stated in the first screen, with his name attached to it. Rhett already has the equivalent shape in his headline and should keep it.
- Section structure: Hero with a short bio paragraph, then Videos and Interviews, then Special Projects and Deep Dives, then an About page carrying press, book, and keynote links. It is a hub with subpages, not a one pager, but the homepage itself stays short.
- Proof and trust signals: Named media (Prof G Pod with Scott Galloway, Real Vision, Plain English with Derek Thompson, The Compound), named outlets for his writing (The New York Times, The Atlantic, The Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg), two books (Rethinking Real Estate, 2019, and a forthcoming Pan Macmillan book), and institutions where he has spoken (Wharton, MIT, Columbia, LSE credential). Proof is specific and third party, never self graded.
- Content engine: A newsletter called Old/New with "Join 30,000+ subscribers in finance, tech, and beyond" as the capture line on the homepage, a predictions page that keeps a public track record, and the book as the long form anchor. The content lives in the newsletter and books. The site is the index and the proof wall.
- Conversion and contact path: Subscribe is the primary conversion. Speaking invitations are the secondary path, handled on a keynotes page. No services menu on the homepage and no contact form in the hero.
- Email capture: Yes, prominent, and the subscriber count does double duty as a trust signal.
- Design and credibility feel: Quiet, editorial, text led, one strong portrait. It feels like a writer's site, not a funnel.
- Steal: (a) The proof wall pattern, where third party names do the credibility work in a compact strip. For Rhett this means industry rooms and outlets rather than clients: CREDA, CoreNet New England, ULI Boston, and Boston Capital Strategy as his publication. (b) The named-territory discipline, one idea with his name on it. (c) A track record habit, keeping dated public positions in one place so a name search finds a record, not a claim. His version lives on Boston Capital Strategy (the Lindemann-Hurley assessment, the BERDO guide), and the personal site should point at it as a record, not restate it. (d) The interview and appearances index as a format he can grow into.
- Skip deliberately: The newsletter and its email capture (his writing and any list belong to Boston Capital Strategy, and a personal list at a Deloitte employee raises independence questions he does not need). The keynote sales page and paid speaking posture (he is building a partner track inside a firm, not selling talks). The subscriber count as social proof (he has no number that survives honesty yet, and inventing scale is exactly the failure mode BRIEF.md bans). Book framing (nothing to announce).
- Employer risk note: Poleg is independent. Nothing on his site needs an employer's permission. The patterns transfer, the business model does not.

## 2. Antony Slumbers, antonyslumbers.com

Verdict: A masterclass in owning a named idea and in speaker proof, wrapped around a commercial engine (a paid course, consulting, paid speaking) that is almost entirely off limits to Rhett. Steal the naming and the proof craft. Skip the store.

- Positioning and headline: The homepage is currently led by his course, "#GenerativeAIForRealEstatePeople, The Trillion Dollar Hashtag". Under it sits the durable positioning: "globally recognised speaker, advisor and writer on PropTech and space-as-a-service", plus the coined term #SpaceAsAService, which he dates to 2014 and has repeated for a decade. The lesson is that a coined, repeated phrase becomes a search asset and a memory hook.
- Section structure: Course block first, then a welcome bio, then four doors: About me, The Blog, Speaking, Consulting, each with an image and a Learn More link. The About page carries a full dated career timeline from 1988 to a May 2026 course launch, including exits, rankings, and advisory roles.
- Proof and trust signals: Named clients and institutions (CBRE, Hines, British Land, Oxford Properties, Tishman Speyer, EPRA, INREV, CoreNet, RICS), named testimonials with names and titles, 19 cohorts and named rankings on the About timeline (Top 20 PropTech influencer, Duke Long lists, a 2024 UK PropTech Association special achievement award). The speaking page is a long evidence file of events, photos, and attributed quotes.
- Content engine: A blog running since 2013, a monthly Estates Gazette column since 2014, courses as the monetized form of the same material, and a podcast launched in 2020. One territory, many formats, a decade of compounding.
- Conversion and contact path: Course registration at a stated price (645 pounds per person), a contact page for speaking and consulting proposals, and testimonial density doing the persuading.
- Email capture: Not the homepage engine. The course and the contact page carry conversion. The blog is the long running top of funnel.
- Design and credibility feel: Dense and dated in places (Squarespace, long image heavy pages), but the density itself signals a long record. Credibility comes from accumulation, not polish.
- Steal: (a) The coined phrase discipline. Rhett's equivalent asset is a short, repeated framing for his niche (capital renewal, what gets funded next) used consistently across Boston Capital Strategy and his X posts, so a name search and a phrase search start to meet. (b) The dated timeline as a format. A short dated record of talks, panels, published pieces, and industry roles is the one proof format that works at his stage, because it grows by accretion and every line is checkable. (c) Attributed third party quotes over self description, when he has them with permission.
- Skip deliberately: Everything with a price tag or a proposal path: the course, the consulting page, paid speaking, client name lists, and testimonial walls naming employers. For a Deloitte consultant each of those reads as a competing practice or a client disclosure problem, and BRIEF.md bans all of them. Also skip the volume strategy: a blog since 2013 is his business, while Rhett's writing has one home already.
- Employer risk note: Slumbers' site is the clearest example of what Rhett's must never look like: an independent advisory storefront with named clients. The distance between the two sites is a feature to protect, not a gap to close.

## 3. James Dearsley, jamesdearsley.co.uk

Verdict: The useful lesson is role clarity on a small site: three named roles, a compact awards record, and a personal About page that separates the man from the offer. The cautionary lesson is staleness: parts of the record stop in 2022, and a thin record left unmaintained argues against its owner.

- Positioning and headline: "Transformational leader. Author. Keynote Speaker. Professional Conference Emcee." Four role labels up front, then three role sections (Speaker and Moderator, NED and Advisor, Futurist and Community Leader). A visitor knows what he is for within one screen.
- Section structure: Homepage with a pull quote testimonial, an awards strip, the three role sections with photos, then About and NED and Advisory subpages. The About page leads with family and personal interests (gardening, beekeeping, sport), then tells the career story by decade (2010s PropTech, 2020s Web 3.0 and metaverse), which is a memorable narrative device.
- Proof and trust signals: A compact dated awards list (2016 No. 1 Global PropTech Influencer, LendInvest; 2019 Commentator of the Year, Property Press Awards; 2022 Global PropTech Power List, Commercial Observer; 2022 Global PropTech Influencer, PropTech LATAM), one strong attributed testimonial at the top, and the Unissu co-founder role as the operating credential.
- Content engine: Community building rather than publishing volume: he created the original PropTech community in the early 2010s and holds a role at Unissu. The site is a brochure for speaking, emcee, and advisory work, fed by reputation earned in rooms.
- Conversion and contact path: Booking oriented. The speaker directory profile states plainly that booking information is at this site, and the NED page asks directly whether he can support your company. Conversion is the point of the site.
- Email capture: None visible on the pages read. No newsletter push. The contact and booking path replaces capture.
- Design and credibility feel: HubSpot built, photo led, clean at first read, but the awards stop at 2022 and the decade narrative leans on a Web 3.0 bet that dates the page. It reads as a strong record that has not been kept current.
- Steal: (a) Role labels stated plainly and early. Rhett's one page already does a version of this in the Introduction and can sharpen it without adding sections. (b) A compact, dated record strip, where each line is an award, a talk, a byline, or a role with a year, rather than a paragraph of claims. (c) The personal first About move: his Outside work section does the same job in miniature, and Dearsley shows it builds warmth without oversharing.
- Skip deliberately: The booking and NED sales posture (a services offer by another name). The emcee and speaker for hire framing while employed. The by decade predictions narrative, which needs a decade of public bets he has not made yet. Named family detail (BRIEF.md bans identifiable information about other people, and his Outside work section stays at interests, not people).
- Employer risk note: Dearsley shows the failure mode of a record page that stops being maintained. For Rhett, any record he starts must be cheap to keep current inside the weekly scrum, or it should not start.

## Cross-site patterns

What all three share, and what transfers to a one-page static site at Rhett's stage:

1. One named territory, repeated for years. Poleg and AI and work, Slumbers and space as a service, Dearsley and PropTech community. Rhett's is capital planning and deferred maintenance in Boston, already set. The transfer is consistency of phrasing, not new content.
2. Proof is third party and dated. Outlets, institutions, rankings, and attributed quotes, each with a name or a year. The personal site version is a compact record strip fed by real events (rooms he speaks in, pieces published, roles held), never client names.
3. The site is an index, not the engine. Newsletters, books, blogs, and courses live elsewhere and the site points at them. Rhett's elsewhere is Boston Capital Strategy, which is exactly the Elsewhere section's job. Strengthen the pointing, do not duplicate the engine.
4. A speaking and appearances record is the highest value growth surface for all three. At Rhett's stage the honest version is small: panels, breakfasts, and industry rooms he actually appears in, logged as they happen.
5. Email capture is optional, not obligatory. Only Poleg runs it as the primary conversion, and his is a publishing business. Dearsley converts without any capture. Rhett should skip capture on the personal site.

What none of them have to manage, and Rhett does: an employer. Every commercial element on these sites (course prices, consulting pages, client lists, booking paths, paid tiers) is closed to him by BRIEF.md, and several would create a competing practice problem rather than a marketing win. The winning move is to borrow their proof architecture and naming discipline while keeping his site visibly non-commercial.
